## What does a reconstruction contractor do after mitigation is finished?

A reconstruction contractor rebuilds what mitigation removed, framing, insulation, drywall, flooring, cabinets, trim, and paint, starting after the water or fire crew finishes drying, demo, and cleanup. Mitigation stops the damage from spreading; reconstruction is the build-back phase that gets the space livable again.

On a typical water loss, that might mean replacing a section of wall framing, hanging and finishing new drywall, and reinstalling flooring. On a fire loss, it can be a full room rebuild including cabinetry and paint matching. Our [reconstruction services page](/services/reconstruction/) walks through the scope in more detail, and our earlier post on [what reconstruction means after water or fire damage](/blog/reconstruction/) covers the basic phases if you want the fuller picture.

## Should you hire one company for mitigation and the rebuild, or two separate contractors?

Using one company for both phases usually means fewer handoffs, one scope of work, and one company accountable to your insurance adjuster from the first call to the final walkthrough. Splitting the job between a mitigation company and a separate general contractor can work, but it adds a seam where responsibility, and sometimes the paper trail, can get lost.

If you do use two contractors, make sure the mitigation company's final documentation, drying logs, moisture readings, demo photos, gets handed directly to whoever does the rebuild. That record is what your adjuster uses to approve the reconstruction line items, and gaps in it slow the claim down.

## What credentials should a reconstruction contractor in Farrell, PA actually have?

At minimum, check for Pennsylvania Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act (HICPA) registration, proof of general liability insurance, and a willingness to put the full scope of work in writing before any demo begins. Most residential home improvement work in Pennsylvania legally requires this registration, and the [PA Attorney General's home improvement page](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/businesses-and-organizations/home-improvement-contractor-registration/) explains what it covers and how to verify a contractor.

## How does a reconstruction contractor work with your insurance adjuster?

A reconstruction contractor who's used to insurance work writes the rebuild scope in Xactimate, the line-item estimating software most carriers use, so the numbers match what your adjuster expects to see. That shared format is what gets a supplemental claim approved faster instead of bounced back for clarification.

In practice, that means photos tied to specific line items, material and labor pricing that reflects current local rates, and a scope that separates what mitigation already paid for from what the rebuild still needs. If your adjuster has questions about why a wall needs full replacement instead of patching, the contractor should be able to answer directly from the documentation, not guess after the fact.

## What's a realistic timeline from tear-out to move-back-in?

Most single-room rebuilds, drywall, paint, and flooring, typically run one to three weeks once materials are on hand, while a full kitchen or multi-room rebuild after a larger loss often takes four to eight weeks depending on cabinet lead times and permit requirements. These are general industry ranges, not a quote, since every loss involves different materials, square footage, and how fast insurance approves the scope.

Ask any contractor you're considering for a written timeline tied to their actual scope of work, not a verbal estimate. Lead times on cabinets and specialty flooring are usually the biggest variable, not the labor itself.

## What red flags should you watch for when hiring a reconstruction contractor?

The two biggest red flags are pressure to sign an assignment of benefits (AOB) before you understand what you're signing away, and a demand for a large deposit before any work or materials are committed. An AOB hands your insurance claim rights to the contractor, which can limit your ability to dispute the settlement later, and it's worth reading closely or having your insurer explain before you sign.

Other warning signs: no written scope of work, no proof of HICPA registration when asked, and a contractor who won't put a start date or timeline in writing. A legitimate contractor will walk you through the scope, the materials, and the payment schedule before asking for anything upfront.
