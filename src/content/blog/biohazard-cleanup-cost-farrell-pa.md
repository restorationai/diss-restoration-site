## What Counts as Biohazard Cleanup?

Biohazard cleanup covers any situation involving blood, bodily fluids, or human and animal waste that poses an infection risk. That includes unattended deaths, suicides, violent crime scenes, and severe sewage backups that bring contaminated water into living spaces.

These jobs are different from routine cleaning because the materials involved can carry bloodborne pathogens. [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org/) training and the [OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens standard](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens) both govern how technicians handle, contain, and dispose of this kind of contamination. [Biohazard cleanup](/services/biohazard-cleanup/) and [trauma scene cleanup](/services/trauma-scene-cleanup/) are related but not identical: trauma scenes often involve more structural demolition, while general biohazard calls can include sewage or unattended-death situations with less physical damage.

## What Changes the Price?

The biggest cost driver is time: how long fluids or tissue sat before cleanup began. The longer contamination has contact with flooring, subfloor, and wall materials, the more has to come out rather than be disinfected in place.

Other factors include:

- **Square footage affected**, including whether contamination spread to adjoining rooms or down through floor joists
- **Porous materials involved**, like carpet, padding, drywall, and upholstered furniture, which usually can't be sanitized and must be removed
- **Odor remediation**, often requiring ozone or hydroxyl treatment after visible contamination is gone
- **Disposal volume**, since regulated medical waste is billed separately from standard demolition debris
- **Access and containment needs**, such as setting up negative air pressure to protect the rest of the home

A small bathroom incident on tile and non-porous surfaces might stay near the bottom of the range. A bedroom with carpet, a mattress, and several weeks of decomposition moves toward the top.

## Why Can't You Just Clean This Yourself?

Biohazard waste can't go in household trash because it may carry bloodborne pathogens like hepatitis B, hepatitis C, or HIV, and most municipal waste systems aren't equipped to handle it safely.

Regulated medical waste has to be packaged, transported, and destroyed by a licensed hauler, following standards referenced in [OSHA's bloodborne pathogens guidance](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens). This is also why DIY cleanup is risky: standard gloves and disinfectant wipes don't protect against pathogens that can live on surfaces for days, and improper handling can spread contamination to parts of the home that were never affected. ## Will Insurance Cover Biohazard Cleanup?

Homeowners and landlord policies sometimes cover biohazard cleanup when it follows a covered event, like an accidental death or a crime, but coverage varies widely by policy and insurer.

Most standard policies were written with property damage in mind, not biohazard remediation specifically, so it's worth asking your agent directly whether cleanup, contents disposal, and odor treatment are included. The [Insurance Information Institute's overview of standard homeowners coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) is a useful starting point for understanding what a typical policy does and doesn't cover. Keep photos, an itemized scope of work, and all receipts; that documentation is what an adjuster will ask for regardless of the outcome.
