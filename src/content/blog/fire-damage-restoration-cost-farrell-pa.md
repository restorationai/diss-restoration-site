## What Drives Fire Restoration Costs Up?

The biggest cost drivers are soot type, how far smoke traveled, and how much structural material has to be removed instead of cleaned. Those three factors matter more than total square footage.

**Soot type.** Protein soot from a kitchen grease fire is nearly invisible but has an intense, sticky odor that spreads through grout lines and HVAC systems, often requiring more odor-removal work than cleaning. Dry smoke soot from a fast, hot fire (paper, wood) wipes off more easily. Wet smoke soot from a slow, smoldering fire involving synthetics (furniture foam, plastics) smears and penetrates porous materials, which usually means more gets torn out rather than cleaned.

**Smoke travel.** If the HVAC system ran during the fire, soot and odor can spread to rooms that never saw flame. That turns a one-room job into a whole-house [smoke damage restoration](/services/smoke-damage-restoration/) job, which raises both labor and equipment hours.

**Structural loss.** Charred studs, burned-through roof decking, or compromised drywall can't just be cleaned; they have to come out and get rebuilt to code. That shifts cost from cleaning rates (per square foot, lower) to [reconstruction](/services/reconstruction/) rates (per square foot, higher, and tied to lumber and labor pricing at the time of the job).

**Contents affected.** Furniture, electronics, clothing, and personal items that survived the fire but took on smoke odor typically get inventoried and either cleaned off-site or deemed a total loss. [Contents restoration](/services/contents-restoration/) costs are usually billed separately from the structural scope, which is why two fires of the same size can have very different total bills depending on what was in the room.

Older Mercer County housing stock, including a lot of the balloon-frame construction common in Farrell's steel-era neighborhoods, can also mean smoke and heat traveled further through wall cavities than they would in newer, more compartmentalized framing, which sometimes adds hidden scope once walls are opened up.

## Does Insurance Pay the Full Restoration Cost?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental fire damage as a named peril, but "covered" doesn't automatically mean "paid in full." Your deductible, policy limits, and documentation all affect the final number you're responsible for.

Fire is one of the perils typically included on a standard homeowners policy, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance), but coverage details, including whether code-upgrade costs or additional living expenses are included, vary by carrier and policy. You'll pay your deductible regardless, and if the restoration scope exceeds your dwelling coverage limit, the difference comes out of pocket.

Documentation matters more than people expect. Photos of the damage before cleanup starts, a detailed room-by-room inventory of affected contents, and a written restoration scope from the company doing the work all strengthen the claim. Most restoration companies that regularly work fire claims will coordinate directly with your adjuster and bill the approved scope to the carrier, so the homeowner typically isn't fronting the full restoration cost, just the deductible.

## What Does the Fire Damage Restoration Process Actually Involve?

Fire restoration generally moves through board-up, inspection, cleaning, structural drying if water was used to fight the fire, odor treatment, and rebuild, roughly in that order. Skipping steps or doing them out of sequence is one of the fastest ways a job goes over budget.

1. **Emergency board-up and tarping.** Broken windows and roof openings get secured the same day to stop weather and theft exposure from adding a second loss on top of the fire.
2. **Inspection and scope.** A technician documents every affected area, soot type, and smoke path, and writes the scope the insurance claim will be based on.
3. **Soot and smoke cleaning.** Surfaces get cleaned using techniques matched to the soot type; this follows the [ANSI/IICRC S700 standard](https://iicrc.org/s700/) for fire and smoke restoration.
4. **Structural drying.** If firefighting water soaked framing or flooring, that water has to come out and the structure has to dry before rebuild starts, or mold becomes a second problem.
5. **Odor removal.** Thermal fogging, hydroxyl generators, or ozone treatment address smoke odor trapped in porous materials and HVAC ductwork. Our [fire damage restoration process breakdown](/blog/fire-damage-restoration-process/) walks through this stage in more detail.
6. **Reconstruction.** Charred framing, drywall, flooring, and finishes get rebuilt to current code.
