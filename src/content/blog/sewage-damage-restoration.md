## What Does Sewage Damage Restoration Actually Involve?

Sewage damage restoration means extracting contaminated water, removing materials that can't be disinfected, applying antimicrobial treatment, and drying the structure to the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), a process that typically takes 3 to 7 days depending on how far the water traveled and what it touched.

The work usually runs in this order: contaminated water gets extracted first, using equipment dedicated to Category 3 losses and never mixed with clean-water jobs. Porous materials that absorbed sewage, carpet, carpet pad, drywall up to the waterline, baseboards and often subflooring, get removed and discarded rather than dried in place, because they can't be fully disinfected. Non-porous surfaces like concrete, tile and sealed wood get cleaned and treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial. Air movers and dehumidifiers then run until moisture readings in the studs and subfloor return to normal, which a technician checks with a moisture meter rather than guessing by feel or smell.

## Why Can't I Just Clean Up a Sewage Backup Myself?

Category 3 water contains sewage, bacteria and sometimes chemical contaminants, and the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) calls for containment, PPE and antimicrobial treatment most households don't have on hand, which is why DIY cleanup often spreads contamination instead of removing it.

The health risk is the real issue. Sewage can carry E. coli, hepatitis A and intestinal parasites, and a wet shop vac or a mop bucket becomes a vector that tracks contamination into rooms that were never affected. The [OSHA guidance on flood and water cleanup](https://www.osha.gov/flood) outlines why contaminated-water work calls for gloves, eye protection and respiratory protection, not a towel and a window fan. Porous materials are the other problem: once sewage soaks into drywall or carpet pad, cleaning the surface doesn't remove what's inside it, so those materials have to come out.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Sewage Cleanup?

Most standard homeowners policies exclude sewer and drain backups unless you've added a water backup, sometimes called a sewer or sump endorsement, a separate rider that typically runs $50 to $100 a year and caps payouts somewhere between $5,000 and $25,000.

Without that endorsement, a sewer backup is usually treated as a maintenance issue, not a covered peril, even though it feels exactly as sudden and disruptive as a burst pipe. Check your declarations page for a line item mentioning water backup or sump overflow before assuming anything is covered. The [Insurance Information Institute's guide to what standard homeowners insurance covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) breaks down how backup coverage differs from the base policy, and our own [guide to homeowners insurance and water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through documentation that helps either way. Photograph the damage before any cleanup starts and keep every receipt; adjusters lean heavily on timestamped evidence for Category 3 claims.

## What Should I Do in the First Hour After a Sewage Backup?

In the first hour, keep people and pets out of the affected area, shut off the water source if one is actively feeding the backup, open windows for ventilation if weather allows, and call a restoration company experienced in Category 3 water before you touch anything.

Don't run fans aimed at the standing water, since that can aerosolize contaminants into the air you're breathing. Don't flush more water down the affected drain while you're trying to diagnose the source. Take photos of the standing water and any items it touched before cleanup begins, since that documentation protects an insurance claim either way.
