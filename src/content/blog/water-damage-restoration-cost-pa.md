In Pennsylvania, water damage restoration typically costs $1,200 to $7,500 for a contained residential loss, and $7,000 to $15,000 or more when sewage, flooding, or extensive structural drying is involved. The biggest swing factor isn't square footage, it's water category: clean water from a burst supply line costs far less to remediate than water carrying sewage or flood contamination.

If you're standing in a wet basement or living room in Mercer County or the Shenango Valley right now trying to get a number before you call anyone, here's the real pricing picture, what moves it, and what your homeowners policy is likely to pay for.

## What Does Water Damage Restoration Cost in Pennsylvania?

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Water extraction only, under 500 sq ft, clean water | $400 - $1,000 |
| Minor water damage, single room, clean water (Cat 1) | $1,200 - $3,500 |
| Moderate damage, multiple rooms, drywall and flooring removal | $3,500 - $7,500 |

These are typical industry ranges for Pennsylvania, not a quote. Every loss is different, and a written scope after an on-site inspection is the only way to know what your specific job will cost.

## What Factors Actually Move the Price?

The water's category and the amount of material that has to be removed, not demolished and dried, drive most of the cost difference between a $1,500 job and a $10,000 one.

**Water category.** Under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), Category 1 is clean water from a sanitary source like a supply line. Category 2 (gray water) includes washing machine discharge or a toilet overflow with urine but no feces. Category 3 (black water) includes any sewer or drain backup from beyond the trap, a toilet overflow with feces, and flood or storm surge water. Category 2 and 3 losses usually require removing porous materials like carpet pad and drywall rather than drying them in place. Category 3 losses additionally require antimicrobial treatment and PPE, which adds labor and disposal cost.

**Square footage and materials affected.** A single bathroom leak costs far less than water that traveled under hardwood flooring across a first floor. Hardwood, plaster, and concrete are Class 4 materials under S500 and need specialty low-grain-refinement (LGR) drying equipment run longer, which adds to the bill.

**How fast the water gets stopped and extracted.** Mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home). A delayed response often turns a drying job into a drying-plus-mold-remediation job.

**Code upgrades during repair.** If drywall, insulation, or electrical has to be brought up to current code during reconstruction, that's a separate line item from the drying and mitigation cost.

## Will My Homeowners Insurance Cover It?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a pipe that bursts or an appliance hose that suddenly fails, but they exclude gradual leaks, wear and tear, and poor maintenance, according to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).

Two common gaps catch people off guard. Flood damage, meaning surface water, overflowing creeks, or storm surge, is never covered by a standard homeowners policy; it requires a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood carrier. And sewer or drain backups, along with sump pump failure, are only covered if you've added a water backup endorsement; without it, a backed-up floor drain is typically an out-of-pocket repair.

Documentation protects your claim either way. Photograph the damage before anything is moved, keep every receipt, and get a written scope of work from whoever handles the mitigation. ## What Does the Restoration Process Actually Involve?

A standard water damage job moves through five phases, and understanding them explains where the cost comes from.

1. **Inspection and moisture mapping.** A technician uses a moisture meter and sometimes a thermal camera to map how far the water traveled, including behind walls and under flooring.
2. **Water extraction.** Truck-mounted or portable extractors pull standing water and saturated carpet pad before drying begins.
3. **Controlled demolition.** Any material that can't be dried in place (soaked drywall below the flood line, ruined insulation, contaminated carpet) gets removed.
4. **Structural drying.** Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run for several days, with daily moisture readings to confirm materials are drying on schedule and to IICRC S500 targets.
5. **Cleaning, sanitizing, and reconstruction.** Affected surfaces are cleaned and treated, and any removed materials are replaced to restore the space.
