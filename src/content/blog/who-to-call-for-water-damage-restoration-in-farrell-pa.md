## Do You Call a Restoration Company or a Plumber First?

Call a restoration company first when water has already soaked flooring, drywall, or insulation, even if the source itself is handled. Call a plumber first, or alongside the restoration call, when the water is still actively running and something needs to be shut off, repaired, or replaced at the source.

A burst supply line behind a wall is a good example: the valve needs to come off first (sometimes the main shutoff for the whole house), but the water already on the floor and inside the wall cavity needs professional extraction and drying equipment, not a wet-vac and a box fan. That water starts as Category 1, or clean water, under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), but it degrades the longer it sits against drywall, subfloor, and insulation. A related situation, a sewer or floor drain backup, is never clean water: it's Category 3 (black water) and needs antimicrobial treatment and specific PPE, not a towel and bleach. If the backup is coming from a toilet or floor drain, skip the DIY cleanup entirely.

If your basement floods from groundwater seeping through the foundation during heavy rain, that's a different call. See our guide on a [flooded basement in Farrell](/blog/flooded-basement-who-to-call/) for how that triage works, since it often involves a sump pump question insurance treats differently than a burst pipe.

## What Happens When You Call a Restoration Company?

The first call gets a technician scheduled and starts documentation that protects your insurance claim, usually within the same conversation. Have the type of water (clean, appliance discharge, backup), the affected rooms, and whether the source is still running ready to describe.

On arrival, the crew checks for safety hazards first (standing water near outlets, saturated ceiling that could sag), then uses a moisture meter to map how far the water traveled behind walls and under flooring, often farther than it looks from the surface. Extraction starts immediately with truck-mounted or portable extractors, followed by air movers and low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers positioned to dry the structure in place where possible. Photos and moisture readings get logged at each visit; that log is what an adjuster uses to verify the scope of loss, not a guess at how bad it looked on day one. ## When Is It a Gas or Electrical Hazard Instead?

Call 911 or the utility company first if you smell gas, see sparking outlets, or water is pooling near an electrical panel. No restoration crew should enter a space with an active gas leak or exposed live wiring until the utility has made it safe. Once the hazard is cleared, the water damage call comes next.

A tripped breaker that won't reset, or outlets below the waterline in a flooded basement, are reasons to stay out of the room and call the utility company before anyone starts moving furniture or wet-vacuuming.

## Does Insurance Cover the Water Damage, and Who Handles That?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a pipe that bursts or an appliance hose that suddenly fails, but they typically exclude gradual leaks and long-term seepage. Sewer backups and sump pump failures are usually NOT covered unless the policy has a specific water backup endorsement added, so check your declarations page before assuming either is included (see the [Insurance Information Institute's guide to homeowners coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance)).

Homeowners also have a duty to mitigate further damage once the water stops, meaning prompt drying isn't optional if you want the claim paid in full. A restoration crew's moisture logs and photo documentation are exactly what an adjuster needs to approve the scope without a dispute. For more on what's typically covered and what's not, see [Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/)

If the water has already spread past a single room, [request a water damage assessment](/services/water-damage-restoration/) rather than waiting to see if it dries on its own. The longer wet materials sit, the more likely drywall, flooring, or subfloor need replacement instead of drying.
