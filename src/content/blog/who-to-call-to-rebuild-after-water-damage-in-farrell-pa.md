## Who should you call when it's time to rebuild?

The rebuild call is different from the emergency call. If water is still flowing (a pipe still spraying, a drain still backing up), that's an emergency water removal problem first, not a rebuild problem. Once the structure is dry and any mold risk has been cleared, that's when a reconstruction crew takes over.

If you're still in the active water removal stage, see our [burst pipe guide](/blog/burst-pipe-in-farrell-who-to-call-first/) or [flooded basement guide](/blog/flooded-basement-who-to-call/) first. This post picks up after drying is finished, when the question shifts from "who stops the water" to "who puts the house back together."

## When does drying end and rebuilding start?

Drying ends when moisture readings on the affected studs, subfloor, and framing return to a dry standard, generally within a few points of the same materials in an unaffected part of the house. A technician checks this with a moisture meter, not by eye or by feel; damp-looking wood can read dry, and dry-looking drywall can still be wet behind the surface.

Until those readings hold steady across more than one visit, closing the wall back up risks trapping moisture that leads to rot or mold later. The [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) is the industry benchmark restoration crews work against for this.

## What gets torn out versus what gets saved?

Drywall and insulation that got wet usually get cut out and replaced, not dried in place, because wet insulation loses its R-value and wet drywall wicks moisture upward long after the surface looks fine. Solid wood subfloor, cabinets, and trim are judged case by case.

Solid wood subfloor can often be dried and saved if it was caught early and the water was clean. Particleboard subfloor, and anything that swelled or delaminated, typically gets replaced. Cabinets get the same test: solid wood boxes with minimal swelling can sometimes stay; MDF or particleboard cabinet bases that absorbed water usually don't survive drying and get rebuilt.

## Why do mold checks come before new drywall?

Because mold can start growing on wet material within 24 to 48 hours, and closing new drywall over an active mold problem just hides it. The [EPA notes](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home) that any mold growth found during the open-wall period needs to be addressed before the wall goes back up, not after.

A quick visual and moisture check at the open-stud stage is the cheapest point in the whole job to catch a mold problem. If growth is found, [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) happens before framing and drywall resume, which is faster and cheaper than tearing a finished wall back open later.

## How does insurance handle the rebuild portion of the claim?

The rebuild is usually a separate line item on the claim from the mitigation (drying) work, and most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage like a burst pipe, including the cost to repair what was removed, but check your specific policy since coverage varies by insurer. A written scope of the rebuild, with photos of the open-stud stage and the moisture readings that justified the tear-out, is what gives the adjuster something concrete to approve against.

Keep every receipt and every moisture log from the drying phase. That documentation is what separates a rebuild estimate an adjuster approves quickly from one that gets questioned line by line. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) has a plain-English rundown of what standard policies typically cover and what they don't.

## Do you need a permit to rebuild in Farrell or Mercer County?

Likely yes for anything beyond cosmetic patching, like replacing structural framing, moving electrical, or re-running plumbing. Straight drywall and insulation replacement in a like-for-like repair is often exempt, but once framing, electrical, or plumbing is touched, Mercer County's building code office typically requires a permit before work closes the walls back up.

A reconstruction crew that pulls permits as part of the job keeps this off your plate. It also protects resale value later, since an unpermitted structural repair can surface as a problem during a future home inspection or sale.

## What happens when you call, and how long does the rebuild take?

On the first call, have your insurance claim number, the moisture logs or drying report from the mitigation phase, and a rough sense of which rooms are affected. A project manager walks the open structure, confirms it's cleared for close-up, and writes a scope covering framing, insulation, drywall, trim, flooring, and paint.

A single room, like a bathroom or a small section of basement, typically rebuilds in one to two weeks once materials are ordered. A multi-room loss with cabinetry, flooring, and permitted structural work can run four to eight weeks. The biggest variable isn't labor, it's how fast the insurance scope gets approved and materials get ordered, so getting that documentation right early matters more than almost anything else in the timeline.
