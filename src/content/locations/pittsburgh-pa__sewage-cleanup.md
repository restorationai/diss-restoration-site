## Why Pittsburgh Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

Much of Pittsburgh runs on a combined sewer system, meaning stormwater and sanitary waste share the same pipes under large sections of the city and surrounding Allegheny County boroughs. During heavy rain, those lines can surcharge, and the water finds the lowest point in a building, usually a basement floor drain or a laundry hookup. Add in the region's hilly topography, where older homes sit below street grade on steep lots, and gravity does the rest of the work pushing backed-up sewage into finished basements. Pittsburgh's housing stock also skews old: many neighborhoods were built out during the steel era with clay or cast iron lateral lines that have been in the ground for seventy years or more, and tree root intrusion or pipe collapse is a routine cause of a sewer line backup regardless of weather. We also see septic overflow cleanup calls from properties further out in Allegheny County that rely on aging private systems rather than municipal sewer.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Pittsburgh

Every call starts with containment. We isolate the affected area, identify the contamination source, and classify the water under IICRC S500 guidance before anyone touches a wet-vac. Raw sewage removal means pulling standing water, saturated carpet, and in most cases porous materials like drywall and subfloor that have been in contact with black water, since those materials cannot be sanitized back to a safe condition. Once the space is stripped to salvageable structure, we apply EPA-registered antimicrobials, run air scrubbers to manage airborne pathogens, and set up structural drying equipment calibrated to Pittsburgh's humid continental climate, where summer humidity and winter cold both slow natural drying if you do not actively manage it. Waste disposal follows Pennsylvania DEP handling requirements for contaminated materials, and we document every step with photos for your insurance file.

## Local note

Allegheny County's consent decree work with ALCOSAN has been steadily separating storm and sanitary lines in parts of the system, but the transition is incomplete, and homes in lower-lying sections of the city still see backups during intense rain that would not happen in a fully separated system. If your basement has flooded with sewage more than once during a storm, it is worth asking your plumber whether a backwater valve is installed on your lateral, since that single fitting prevents most repeat backups in homes on combined lines.
