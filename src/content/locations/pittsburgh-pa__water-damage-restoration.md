## Why Pittsburgh Properties See Water Damage Issues

Much of Pittsburgh's housing stock predates 1950, which means cast iron drain lines, galvanized supply pipes, and plaster walls that behave very differently from modern construction when they get wet. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out over decades and tends to fail without warning, often at a joint hidden inside a wall or under a slab. Add in the region's freeze-thaw winters, where temperatures swing above and below freezing repeatedly through January and February, and exposed or poorly insulated pipe runs in older additions and unheated crawlspaces are at real risk of splitting. Pittsburgh's hilly terrain also means a lot of basements are cut into the hillside itself, which brings groundwater pressure against foundation walls after sustained rain, a slow seep that homeowners sometimes mistake for condensation until the drywall starts to wick.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Pittsburgh

We start by identifying the water category and extent of migration, which in older Pittsburgh homes often means checking behind plaster and lath rather than just pulling baseboards. Extraction comes first, using truck-mount and portable units depending on access, since a lot of these properties have narrow staircases or tight basement entries that limit equipment size. From there we set containment and drying equipment calibrated to the materials involved: plaster releases moisture more slowly than drywall, so a room that reads dry on a moisture meter at the surface can still be holding water in the lath behind it. We document moisture readings daily and follow IICRC S500 drying standards until materials reach a stable, dry baseline, not just until the surface looks fine.

## Local Note

A detail that catches a lot of Pittsburgh homeowners off guard: parts of the city and surrounding municipalities run on combined sewer systems, meaning stormwater and sanitary sewage share the same pipe. During a heavy rain event, that system can surcharge and push wastewater back up through basement floor drains and low-lying fixtures, which turns a routine wet basement into a Category 3 loss requiring different handling, disposal, and PPE than a clean water intrusion. If your basement backs up specifically during or right after a storm rather than from an obvious plumbing failure, that's usually the cause, and it changes how we approach extraction and sanitizing.

Water sitting in a wall cavity or under flooring doesn't wait for a convenient time, and every hour it sits increases the chance that drywall, subfloor, or framing has to be replaced rather than dried.
