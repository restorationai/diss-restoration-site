## Why Pulaski Properties See Water Damage Issues

Pulaski sits in a part of Lawrence County where older farmhouses, converted outbuildings, and mid-century ranch homes sit on a mix of block foundations and partial crawlspaces. Many of these properties rely on private wells and septic systems rather than municipal utilities, which means a failed pressure tank or a cracked well line can run for hours before anyone notices, especially in a seasonal or less-frequently-checked property. Western Pennsylvania's freeze-thaw winters add another layer: pipes run through unheated crawlspaces or exterior walls in older construction, and a hard freeze followed by a quick thaw is a common trigger for split copper and galvanized lines. Clay-heavy subsoil common across this part of the state also drains slowly, so basements and lower-level additions can see groundwater seepage after heavy spring rain even when the roof and gutters are in good shape.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Pulaski

We start with moisture mapping, not guesswork. Before any equipment goes in, our technicians check subfloor, framing, and wall cavities with meters so we know exactly how far the water traveled, which matters in a county where many homes still have full basements and original wood subflooring under newer finishes. Standing water is extracted first, then we isolate the drying chamber and set air movers and dehumidifiers sized to the actual cubic footage of the space, not a flat rule of thumb. Structural drying follows the IICRC S500 standard, with daily moisture readings logged until materials hit dry targets, not just until they look dry. For older plaster-and-lath walls still found in some Pulaski-area farmhouses, we adjust drying time upward since plaster releases moisture more slowly than drywall, and pulling equipment too early just invites a mold problem two weeks later.
