When a window blows out in a storm, a roof section lifts in high wind, or fire crews cut a ventilation hole during suppression, the building is no longer protected from weather, pests, or trespassers. Emergency board-up and tarping is the stopgap between that moment and permanent repair: plywood over openings, reinforced poly tarping over roof damage, and enough structural common sense to know the difference between a quick patch and one that fails in the next thirty-mile-an-hour gust.

## What Emergency Board-Up and Tarping actually involves

This work covers two related but distinct jobs. Board-up means securing broken windows, damaged doors, or breached walls, usually after a break-in, vehicle impact, fire, or wind event, with exterior-grade plywood cut to fit and fastened into solid framing rather than just the window casing. Tarping means covering roof damage, whether from a fallen limb, missing shingles, or a wind-lifted section, with heavy-mil reinforced poly sheeting that's actually anchored down rather than draped and hoped for.

Both are temporary by design. Neither is a repair. The goal is to stop active water intrusion and secure the opening against entry until your roofer, window contractor, or reconstruction crew can do permanent work, which might be days or weeks out depending on material availability and insurance approval. A board-up or tarp done right should hold through multiple weather events in that gap without needing a second visit.

## Our process

1. **Site safety check.** Before anyone climbs a ladder or steps near broken glass, we check for structural instability, exposed electrical, and sharp debris. A roof that's been compromised by fire or storm can have hidden soft spots.
2. **Measure and match material to the damage.** Window and door openings get cut plywood sized to the actual frame, not the visible hole. Roof sections get measured for the tarp size needed to overlap the damaged area by enough margin to handle wind lift.
3. **Secure, don't just cover.** Plywood is screwed into solid framing, not just face-nailed into trim. Tarps are installed with wood battens and fasteners along the edges, not bare nails through the poly, which tear out in the first real wind.
4. **Photo-document before and after.** Every opening is photographed prior to covering and again once secured, which becomes part of the record your adjuster will ask for.
5. **Check in until permanent repair starts.** Tarps and boards get a follow-up look if severe weather is forecast or if the timeline to permanent repair stretches out.

## What separates a good board-up and tarping response from a bad one

The failure point is almost always fasteners and coverage margin. A tarp stapled instead of battened will shred in one good windstorm. A board cut to the glass opening instead of the stud bay will pull loose the first time someone leans on it or a gust catches the edge. Adjusters look for exactly this: was the opening actually sealed, or does the "temporary repair" look like it needs a temporary repair of its own.

The other common miss is not accounting for secondary damage paths, a tarp that covers the main roof hole but ignores a cracked vent boot or lifted ridge cap nearby, so water still gets in six feet from where everyone was looking. A thorough response treats the whole damaged section, not just the obvious hole.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Northwestern Pennsylvania sees a real mix of wind, ice, and lake-effect snow bands moving in off Lake Erie through the winter months, which puts real stress on any temporary roof covering. A tarp that would hold fine in a dry fall wind can fail under the weight of wet, heavy snow if it isn't properly tensioned. Spring brings its own round of thunderstorms and straight-line wind events, the kind that take down tree limbs onto roofs with little warning. Given how much of the Shenango Valley's housing stock is older wood-frame construction, roof decking underneath a tarp is often older lumber that needs a gentler touch when fastening.
