Smoke damage rarely stays where the fire started. Within hours of a kitchen fire, grease fire, or even a neighboring structure fire, acidic soot particles migrate through return air ducts and settle on walls, metal fixtures, and fabric throughout a home. The smell gets worse before it gets better if the wrong products are used, and surfaces that look clean to the eye can still carry a residue film that discolors paint and corrodes metal within days.

## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is not the same job as fire damage reconstruction, even though the two often happen together. It's the targeted removal of soot particles, odor-causing compounds, and residue film from surfaces, HVAC systems, and contents, without necessarily rebuilding structure. The work depends heavily on what burned: protein soot from a stovetop fire behaves differently than synthetic soot from burning plastics or furniture, and wildfire smoke that infiltrated a home with no fire on the property presents its own cleanup profile. Crews use HEPA vacuums, chemical sponges, and specialized cleaning agents matched to the soot type, along with thermal foggers, ozone generators, or hydroxyl machines to break down odor molecules that have bonded to porous materials. Timeline varies: a single-room smoke event from a contained kitchen fire might clear in two to three days, while whole-house smoke infiltration with HVAC contamination can run a week or more once contents cleaning is factored in.

## Our process

1. **Soot characterization.** Technicians identify whether the residue is protein-based (greasy, yellow-brown, pungent), synthetic (black, oily, from plastics and foam), or wildfire particulate (dry, fine, widespread), since each requires a different cleaning agent and approach.
2. **HVAC and duct assessment.** Smoke particles travel through return air and settle inside ductwork. If the system ran during or after the fire, ducts get inspected and cleaned before the system is used again, preventing re-contamination of cleaned rooms.
3. **Surface and content cleaning.** Walls, ceilings, hard surfaces, and salvageable contents are cleaned with dry sponges, wet cleaning, or immersion cleaning depending on material and soot type. Porous items that can't be fully cleaned are documented for the claim.
4. **Odor treatment.** Thermal fogging, ozone treatment, or hydroxyl generators are used to neutralize odor at the molecular level, not just mask it. Ozone requires the space to be unoccupied during treatment; hydroxyl does not.
5. **Sealing and verification.** Where residue has penetrated porous drywall or wood, an odor-blocking sealer is applied before repainting. A final walkthrough confirms both visible residue and odor have been addressed.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke damage like a surface cleaning job and skipping the HVAC system entirely. If ductwork isn't inspected, soot particles recirculate every time the furnace or AC kicks on, and the smell returns weeks later. Another frequent miss is using an all-purpose cleaner on soot without first identifying whether it's protein or synthetic residue; the wrong agent can smear soot deeper into porous surfaces like upholstery or drywall instead of lifting it out. Masking odor with air fresheners or ozone-only treatment without first removing the physical residue is also a shortcut that fails: ozone breaks down odor molecules but doesn't clean soot off a wall. Insurance adjusters evaluating a smoke damage claim typically look for documented moisture and soot readings, photos of affected HVAC components, and an itemized list of contents that required cleaning versus replacement, so thorough documentation at every stage matters for claim approval.
